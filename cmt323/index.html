A flattened world

The Internet and global communications cut the cost of operating and trading worldwide. The result is competition for jobs, markets, resources and ideas, and economies that depend on each other.

The digital firm

Its important business relationships, core processes and key assets are all handled digitally. That gives flexibility: time shifting (work at any time) and space shifting (work from any place).

Why firms invest: six strategic business objectives

1. Operational excellence

Better efficiency gives higher profit.

Walmart: digital links between suppliers and stores; over $524 billion sales in 2019.

2. New products, services, business models

A business model is how a company produces, delivers and sells its products and services.

Apple: iTunes changed music distribution, then iPod, iPhone, iPad.

3. Customer and supplier intimacy

Well-served customers come back and buy more. Close suppliers lower costs.

Mandarin Oriental Hotel tracks guest preferences. JC Penney and its Hong Kong supplier.

4. Improved decision making

Without accurate information, managers rely on forecasts, best guesses and luck, and resources get misplaced.

Verizon: web dashboard with real-time data on complaints, network performance, outages.

5. Competitive advantage

Often the result of achieving the earlier objectives: charge less, perform better, respond faster.

Apple, Walmart, UPS.

6. Survival

Sometimes IT is just the cost of doing business: keep up with rivals, or obey regulations.

Citibank's ATMs. Toxic Substances Control Act, Sarbanes-Oxley Act.

What an information system is

Data

Streams of raw facts.

Information

Data shaped into a form that is meaningful and useful.

Information technology (IT) is the hardware and software a business uses. An information system is a set of connected parts that manage information to support decisions and control, and to help with analysis, visualization and creating products. A computer is only the technical foundation, like the materials and tools used to build a house; the information system is the whole house.

Redrawn from the slide idea. The four activities are input, processing, output and feedback. Feedback returns output to the right people so they can check or correct the input.

The three dimensions: more than computers

Organization

Hierarchy of authority (see the levels below), separate business functions (sales and marketing, human resources, finance and accounting, manufacturing and production), unique business processes, unique culture, and organizational politics.

Management

Managers set the strategy for business challenges. They must also act creatively: create new products and services, and sometimes re-create the organization.

Technology

Computer hardware and software, data management technology, networking and telecommunications (networks, the Internet, intranets, extranets, the World Wide Web). The IT infrastructure is the platform the system is built on.

The slides' UPS example: organization = procedures for tracking packages and managing inventory; management = watching service levels and costs; technology = handheld computers, bar-code scanners, networks and desktop computers.

Why technology alone does not pay

An information system is a tool for creating value. The business information value chain shows raw data being collected, turned into business information and passed on, so that managers can plan, coordinate, control and decide better. The value of a system depends partly on how far it leads to better decisions, higher efficiency and higher profit.

Redrawn. The slide figure shows two groups, business processes and management activities, both feeding firm profitability.

But returns vary a lot. The slide plots firms by IT capital stock against productivity (relative to the industry average): firms that spend similar amounts on IT end up at quite different productivity. The two factors the slides name are adopting the right business model and investing in complementary assets (assets you need before the main investment pays off; example: invest in the technology and in the people to make it work).

Organizational assets

The appropriate business model. Efficient business processes.

Managerial assets

Incentives for management innovation. Teamwork and collaborative work environments.

Social assets

The Internet and telecommunications infrastructure. Technology standards.

Three ways to study information systems

Technical approach

Mathematically based models. Disciplines: computer science, management science, operations research.

Behavioral approach

Behavioral issues such as strategic business integration and implementation. Disciplines: psychology, economics, sociology.

Sociotechnical view (this course)

Best performance comes from jointly optimizing the social and the technical system, which avoids a purely technological approach. Four actors: hardware and software suppliers, business firms, managers and employees, and the firm's environment (legal, social, cultural).

Business processes: how work actually flows

A business process is a logically related set of tasks that defines how a specific piece of business is done; it carries flows of material, information and knowledge. A business can be seen as a collection of processes. A process may sit in one functional area or cut across several, and it can be an asset (done well) or a liability (done badly).

Functional areaExample process (slides)
Manufacturing and productionAssembling the product
Sales and marketingIdentifying customers
Finance and accountingCreating financial statements
Human resourcesHiring employees

Redrawn from the slide's order fulfillment example: one order crosses three departments, so one process involves several functions.

IT makes existing processes more efficient

Automate steps that were manual.

IT enables entirely new processes

Change the flow of information, replace sequential steps with parallel steps, remove delays in decision making, and support new business models.

Enterprise applications: linking the whole firm

Enterprise applications work across functional areas, run business processes across the firm and include all management levels.

Enterprise systems (ERP)

Also called enterprise resource planning. Put data from key business processes into one system and speed up information flow. This gives more flexibility in answering customer requests, more accuracy in order fulfillment, and an overall view of operations for managers.

Supply chain management (SCM)

Manages relationships and shared information (orders, production, inventory) with suppliers, purchasers, distributors and logistics firms. Goal: move the right amount of product from source to consumer as quickly and cheaply as possible. It is an interorganizational system: information flows across organizational boundaries.

Customer relationship management (CRM)

Coordinates sales, marketing and customer service. Goals: optimize revenue, improve satisfaction, increase retention, identify and keep the most profitable customers, increase sales.

Knowledge management (KMS)

Captures and applies knowledge and expertise, makes it available wherever it is needed, and links the firm to outside sources of knowledge.

Platforms. An intranet is an internal network built on Internet standards, often a private part of the company website. An extranet is a company site open only to authorized vendors and suppliers, which helps collaboration. E-business = using digital technology and the Internet to run major business processes. E-commerce = the part of e-business that is buying and selling over the Internet. E-government = delivering information and services to citizens, employees and businesses with Internet technology.

Collaboration and social business

Collaboration can be short or long term, informal or formal (teams). The slides give six reasons it matters more now: the changing nature of work, growth of professional "interaction jobs", changes in how the firm is organized, the changing scope of the firm, the emphasis on innovation, and the changing culture of work.

Social business

Using internal and external social networking platforms to engage employees, customers and suppliers. It aims to deepen interactions and speed up information sharing, needs information transparency, and is seen as a way to raise efficiency, spur innovation and speed up decisions.

Business benefits

Productivity (share knowledge, solve problems), quality (faster fixes), innovation (more ideas), customer service (complaints handled faster), and so better financial performance.

Redrawn. A firm that relies on "command and control" puts no value on teamwork; a collaborative culture has senior managers who rely on teams, and a manager's purpose is to build teams.

Organizations and IT shape each other

The slides say IT and organizations influence each other, and the relationship depends on the organization's structure, business processes, politics, culture, environment and management decisions.

Technical definition

A formal social structure that processes resources from the environment to produce outputs. A formal legal entity with internal rules and procedures, and a social structure.

Behavioral definition

A collection of rights, privileges, obligations and responsibilities, balanced over time through conflict and conflict resolution.

Features of organizations

  • Hierarchical structure
  • Accountability and authority, with impartial decision making
  • The principle of efficiency
  • Routines and business processes
  • Politics, culture, environments, structures

Routines are standard operating procedures: precise rules and practices for almost every expected situation. A business process is a collection of routines, and a firm is a collection of business processes.

Politics

Different viewpoints cause struggle, competition and conflict. Political resistance greatly hampers organizational change.

Culture

Assumptions that define goals and products: what to produce, how and where, and for whom. A strong unifying force, and also a limit on change.

Environments

Organizations and environments affect each other and organizations depend on theirs. Environments change faster than organizations. An information system can act as a lens for scanning the environment.

Disruptive technologies substitute products that perform as well as or better than the existing one, and bring sweeping change (examples: personal computers, smartphones, big data, AI, the Internet). First movers invent them; fast followers have the size and resources to profit from them.

Mintzberg's five structures: entrepreneurial, machine bureaucracy, divisionalized bureaucracy, professional bureaucracy, adhocracy. An information system often reflects the structure. Other features: goals (coercive, utilitarian, normative), constituencies, leadership styles, types of tasks.

What IT does to organizations

Economic impact

IT is a factor of production, like capital and labor, and it changes the cost of information. Transaction costs are the costs of participating in markets. IT lowers them, so it can be worth dealing with other firms (outsourcing) instead of growing the number of employees.

Agency theory

The firm is a web of contracts among self-interested parties that need supervision. Agency costs (the costs of managing and supervising) rise as the firm grows. IT can reduce them, so a firm can grow without adding supervision costs or employees.

Flattening: decisions move to lower levels, fewer managers are needed (IT speeds decisions and widens the span of control), and in postindustrial societies authority rests more on knowledge and competence than on formal position.

Why people resist new systems

Systems affect access to a key resource, information, so they get tangled in organizational politics. They can change structure, culture, politics and work. The slides name four factors: the nature of the innovation, the structure of the organization, the culture of the organization, and the tasks affected.

The Internet raises the accessibility, storage and distribution of information and knowledge and can greatly lower transaction and agency costs (example: a large firm that puts its internal manuals on a website saves millions in distribution).

Before designing a system, consider: the environment; structure (hierarchy, specialization, routines, processes); culture and politics; type of organization and leadership style; the main interest groups affected and users' attitudes; and the tasks, decisions and processes the system will support.

Strategy: Porter's competitive forces

Why do some firms lead their industry? Porter's model gives a general view of a firm, its competitors and its environment. Five forces shape its fate.

ForceWhat the slides say
Traditional competitorsRivals keep creating new products, services, efficiencies and switching costs.
New market entrantsSome industries have high barriers to entry (computer chips). Newcomers have new equipment and younger workers but little brand recognition.
Substitute products and servicesWhat customers might use if your prices rise too high (iTunes as a substitute for CDs).
CustomersCan they switch easily? Can they force firms to compete on price alone in a transparent marketplace?
SuppliersThey have market power when the firm cannot raise its prices as fast as they raise theirs.

Four generic strategies enabled by IT

Low-cost leadership

Produce at a lower price than competitors.

Walmart's efficient customer response system.

Product differentiation

New products or services that greatly change customer convenience and experience. Mass customization; customer experience management.

The slide's example is written "Google Nike".

Focus on market niche

Use information systems for a focused strategy on one niche; specialize.

Hilton Hotels' OnQ system.

Customer and supplier intimacy

Build strong ties and loyalty, and raise switching costs.

Toyota, Amazon.

The Internet threatens some industries (travel agencies, printed encyclopedias, media). The forces still apply but rivalry is stronger, universal standards allow new rivals and entrants, and there are new chances to build brands and loyal customers. Smart products and the Internet of Things (Internet-connected sensors in products such as fitness trackers) widen product differentiation, which can increase rivalry, raise switching costs, discourage new entrants and may reduce suppliers' power.

Industry value chain

The firm's chain is linked to the chains of its suppliers, distributors and customers.

Value web

Independent firms that use tightly synchronized IT to coordinate their chains and make a product or service together. More customer-driven and less linear than a traditional chain.

Synergies

The output of some units is the input of others, or organizations pool markets and expertise.

Bank of NY and JP Morgan Chase; Google buying YouTube.

Core competencies

An activity where the firm is a world-class leader. Relies on knowledge and experience shared across business units.

Procter and Gamble's intranet and directory of experts.

Network economics

The cost of adding a participant is almost zero, with much greater gain. The value of a community grows with its size, and so does software's value as its installed base grows. Contrast with the law of diminishing returns.

Virtual company

Uses networks to ally with other firms and make and distribute products without being limited by organizational boundaries or locations.

Li and Fung manages garment production and shipping and outsources all the work to thousands of suppliers.

Business ecosystems

Industry sets of firms providing related services and products. The slides name platforms (Microsoft, Facebook), keystone firms and niche firms, and say a firm can aim to be a profitable niche player in a larger ecosystem. My reading: keystone firms run the platform.

The catch: strategic systems are hard to keep

Sustaining the advantage

Competitors can retaliate and copy strategic systems, and a system that was an advantage may become a tool for survival.

Aligning IT with business objectives

Do a strategic systems analysis: look at the structure of the industry and at the firm's value chains.

Boss fight

Tap a question to reveal the model answer. Try it in your head first.

1Name Porter's five forces and the four generic IS strategies, each with the slide example.

Forces: traditional competitors, new market entrants, substitute products and services, customers, suppliers.

Strategies: low-cost leadership (Walmart), product differentiation (the slide says "Google Nike"), focus on a market niche (Hilton's OnQ), strengthening customer and supplier intimacy (Toyota, Amazon).

2Explain transaction cost theory and agency theory in one line each, and say what IT does to each cost.
  • Transaction costs are the costs of participating in markets. Firms try to cut them by growing (hiring, buying suppliers). IT lowers them, so working with other firms becomes worthwhile instead of growing headcount.
  • Agency costs are the costs of supervising the people the firm is a web of contracts with. They rise with size. IT lowers them, so a firm can grow without more supervision cost or employees.
3Why do organizations resist new information systems? List the four factors.

Systems control access to information, so they are tied up in politics, and they can change structure, culture and work. The four factors named in the slides: the nature of the innovation, the structure of the organization, its culture, and the tasks affected.

4How does a value web differ from a traditional value chain?

A value chain is the firm's own series of value-adding activities, which can be linked to those of suppliers, distributors and customers (the industry value chain). A value web is a group of independent firms that use highly synchronized IT to coordinate their chains and make a product together. It is more customer-driven and less linear.

5Why does network economics break the usual law of diminishing returns?

In a network the marginal cost of adding a participant is almost zero while the gain is much greater, and the value of a community (or of software with a growing installed base) rises with its size. Traditional economics expects returns to shrink as you add more.

6Walmart (slide case): what problems did it face and how did it respond?

Problems: opportunities from new technology, a large geographic footprint, powerful competition and high costs. Solutions: determine business strategy, design new products and services, integrate multiple lines of business, optimize the in-store experience, and use edge computing. Result: supercenter systems speed ordering and shipping, add in-store services, warehouse for third-party sellers and offer targeted online ads.

7If a strategic system gives a firm an edge, why might the edge disappear, and what should the firm do about it?

Competitors can retaliate and copy the system, and the system may end up as a tool for survival. The slides' advice is to align IT with business objectives by running a strategic systems analysis of the industry structure and the firm's value chains.

Exam traps

  • Information technology is hardware and software; an information system also includes the organization and management dimensions.
  • Data is raw facts; information is data shaped to be useful.
  • The MIS system type (routine reports for middle management) is not the same as MIS the field of study.
  • MIS = routine questions; DSS = nonroutine questions; ESS = senior managers, judgment and outside events.
  • E-commerce is a subset of e-business.
  • Intranet = internal; extranet = authorized outside vendors and suppliers.
  • Complementary assets are organizational, managerial and social. Buying technology without them gives weak returns.
  • IT lowers transaction costs and agency costs. It does not raise them.
  • Value chain = one firm's activities; value web = independent firms working together.
  • Porter's five forces include customers and suppliers, not only competitors.
  • A strategic system is not permanent: competitors can copy it.
