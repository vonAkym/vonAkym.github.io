What IoT is and how it works

Internet of Things (IoT) means the network of physical devices embedded with electronics, software, sensors and network connectivity, so objects can collect and exchange data. The slides describe the flow in four steps: sensor, connectivity, data processing, user interface. A second slide frames the value as connect things, control anything, gain insights, take action. The phrase “Internet of Things” is credited to Kevin Ashton (MIT), from a talk about RFID technology to Procter & Gamble. (The timeline figure shows a few more firsts, such as the smart fridge and the smart watch, but its dates are not clear in the text, so I left them out.)

Design considerations (the eight on the slide)

  • Scalability
  • Reliability and availability
  • Security and privacy
  • Performance
  • Interoperability
  • Power efficiency
  • Cost
  • Maintainability

Interoperability means devices from different makers can work together. The slides use these eight as the checklist for the hospital scenario below.

Embedded system, gateway and wireless choice

Embedded versus general-purpose

An embedded system does one dedicated job inside a larger system, with tightly matched software (firmware), a microcontroller, low power, a minimal interface and a small size. A general-purpose computer runs many tasks, has a full operating system, and uses more power.

IoT gateway

A device that connects IoT devices to the cloud. It aggregates data, translates protocols, gives a secure channel and does some local processing. Benefits: lower delay, less bandwidth (filter and combine before sending), better security by local encryption.

Choosing a wireless technology (slide criteria): range, power use, data rate, delay, topology (point-to-point, star, mesh), cost, scalability, and the environment (indoor or outdoor, interference).

Advantages of IoT (slide)

Less human effort and time; more automation and optimisation; less waste and better use of natural resources.

Disadvantages (slide)

Security of confidential data; network attacks; keeping privacy.

Four challenges: security and privacy (encryption, authentication and access control, privacy rules such as PDPA); interoperability (many standards, need common frameworks); data analytics and AI (big data, predictive machine learning, AI-driven decisions); scalability and management (huge device counts, over-the-air updates, device lifecycle).

What a sensor is

The American National Standards Institute (ANSI) defines a sensor as a device that gives a usable output in response to a specified measurand (the thing being measured). It acquires a physical parameter and converts it into a signal suitable for processing (optical, electrical, mechanical), so a sensor is a transducer. Without sensors there would be no automation. Sensors detect many kinds of phenomena: acoustic (wave amplitude, phase, velocity), electric (charge, voltage, current), optical (refractive index, reflectivity), mechanical (position, velocity, force, pressure), biological/chemical (fluid concentrations), magnetic (field, flux) and thermal (temperature, heat).

The slide lists four physical principles behind sensors: Ampere’s law (a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field feels a force), the Curie-Weiss law (above a transition temperature a ferromagnetic material behaves as paramagnetic), Faraday’s law of induction (a coil resists a change in magnetic field), and the photoconductive effect (light on certain semiconductors lowers their resistance, as in a photoresistor).

Choosing a sensor

Environmental factorsEconomic factorsSensor characteristics
Temperature range, humidity effects, corrosion, size, overrange protection, susceptibility to EM interference, ruggedness, power consumption, self-test capabilityCost, availability, lifetimeSensitivity, range, stability, repeatability, linearity, error, response time, frequency response

Older deck (Chapter 3): a good sensor has accuracy and precision, sensitivity (output change per input change), suitability to its environment, linearity, fast response time, high resolution, selectivity (specific to its target) and low noise sensitivity. Passive sensing detects energy that is naturally available (such as reflected sunlight); active sensing uses its own energy source and measures the reflection (examples on the slide: synthetic aperture radar and laser fluorosensors).

Sensors on the slides

Temperature

Used in buildings, plants, engines, appliances and computers. Temperature can also be measured indirectly through pressure, volume, electrical resistance or strain.

Accelerometer

Measures along one axis and ignores the other directions. Used for vibration, impacts, air bags, washing machines, heart monitors, car alarms.

Gyro

Senses angular velocity: rotation angle change per unit time, in degrees per second. Used in car navigation, stability control, mobile games, camera-shake detection, robots.

Light

Built from a photoresistor, photodiode or phototransistor. Used in cameras, infrared detectors and ambient lighting.

Ultrasonic

The transmitter (trig pin) sends a 40 kHz sound; if it hits an object the sound bounces back to the receiver (echo pin). This is SONAR; the radio version is RADAR. The slide says distance is calculated from time-of-flight. My own working: distance = speed of sound x echo time / 2, because the sound travels there and back.

More modules

The slides show a photo catalogue of Arduino-style sensor modules (soil moisture, flame, PIR motion, DHT11, and others) with no text. See the DHT code in Topic 3.

Actuators: the other half

An actuator produces action or motion. Its input is mainly an electrical signal, air or fluid; its output is a position or a rate, from a few microns to a few metres. The slide’s loop: the sensor reads temperature, the controller decides, the actuator acts.

TypeHow it worksAdvantagesDisadvantages
HydraulicFluid pressure in a cylinder or fluid motor gives linear, rotary or oscillating motion.Very large force and high speed; welding, clamping, lifting vehicles.Leaks, expensive, needs noise reduction, heat exchangers and high maintenance.
PneumaticVacuum or compressed air gives linear or rotary motion.Low cost, works at extreme temperatures, low maintenance, long life, quick start and stop.Pressure loss reduces efficiency; compressor must run all the time; air can be polluted.

Electric examples on the slides: a solenoid is a tightly wound coil that makes a magnetic field when current flows (control valves, car doors). Rotary actuators include DC, AC, stepper and servo motors. Other actuators: heaters (with a temperature sensor and controller), lights (show machine state) and sirens or horns (warn about unattended or dangerous machines). The older deck adds thermal and magnetic actuators (a wax that expands as it warms).

One slide shows the cycle as “the observation” (sensor sees a physical change and gives a low-power electrical output), “the translation” (the microcontroller reads it and triggers a response) and “the action” (a higher-power electrical input becomes a physical change, such as a heater turning on). Two slips on the slides (my note): the electric actuator text sits under a repeated “Pneumatic Actuators” title, and the rotary actuator slide calls a rotary actuator a “pneumatic cylinder” yet lists motors.

Tap a question to reveal the model answer. Try it in your head first.

1Define sensor and actuator, and say how they connect in an IoT system.

A sensor gives a usable output (an electrical signal) in response to a measurand, converting a physical parameter into a signal. An actuator produces action or motion from an input such as an electrical signal, air or fluid. The controller reads the sensor and decides; the usable output of a sensor becomes the input signal for an actuator. Example on the slide: temperature sensor, controller, cooling fan.

2List the three groups of factors for choosing a sensor, with two examples each.

Environmental (temperature range, humidity effects, corrosion, size, ruggedness, power consumption); economic (cost, availability, lifetime); sensor characteristics (sensitivity, range, stability, repeatability, linearity, response time).

3How does an ultrasonic sensor measure distance? Give the formula and a worked example (the formula is my own working).

The trig pin sends a 40 kHz sound; if an object is in the way, it reflects back to the echo pin. The slide says the distance comes from time-of-flight. My working: distance = speed x time / 2 (divide by 2 because the sound goes out and back). Example (my numbers, speed of sound taken as about 343 m/s, not on the slide): an echo time of 0.01 s gives 343 x 0.01 / 2 = 1.715 m.

4Walk through the sensor signal chain from the older deck, and say why each step exists.

Sensing element touches the phenomenon; signal conditioning amplifies, filters and cleans the weak or noisy signal; the ADC samples, quantises and encodes it into digital values; the controller then uses the data. Sampling must be at least twice the highest signal frequency (Nyquist) or aliasing happens.

5Compare hydraulic, pneumatic and electric actuators.

Hydraulic: huge force, but leaks, cost and maintenance. Pneumatic: cheap, durable, quick, but the compressor must keep running and pressure loss costs efficiency. Electric: quiet, leak-free and reprogrammable with the best position control, but expensive and sensitive to environment.

6Name four rotary-motor types and three non-motor actuators from the slides.

DC, AC, stepper and servo motors. Heaters, lights, and sirens or horns. (Solenoids are the linear electric example.)

Part 1: the circuit that powers it

Every electric circuit has three parts: a source of energy, a closed path and a device that uses the energy. If any part is open, the device does not work. Current is the flow of electrons, voltage is the driving force that pushes it, and resistance is anything that slows it. They link through Ohm’s law: V = I x R, which rearranges to I = V / R and R = V / I.

Part 2: the brain, an embedded system

An embedded system is hardware and software that together form a component of a larger machine. What is unique: it is designed for one specific task, runs on its own without human intervention, and must meet real-time performance limits. Examples on the slides: industrial robots, GPS receivers, cameras, DVD players, routers, set-top boxes, game consoles, photocopiers, microwave ovens, refrigerators and car systems.

Microprocessor

Only a CPU, in one or a few chips. It has no RAM, ROM or peripherals of its own and depends on external circuits.

Microcontroller

“A computer on a chip”: CPU, input/output, memory, clock, timer and other peripherals together, running a single program for dedicated tasks.

The slides show an Arduino Uno WiFi, a Raspberry Pi 4 and the Maker Feather AIoT S3 board (an ESP32-S3 board; its pin map refers to the ESP32-S3 datasheets). Older deck: embedded hardware is the microcontroller or microprocessor plus memory (RAM, ROM, flash); software is firmware, middleware and application; interfaces are sensors in, actuators out, and communication interfaces such as UART, I2C and SPI. When choosing a board, check processor speed and power, memory, pins and connectivity, language and IDE support, and community support.

Programming it (Arduino, in C)

A sketch has two main functions: setup() runs once after power-up or reset (set variables, pin modes, libraries) and loop() runs again and again to respond. Variables can be local (inside a function), formal parameters, or global (outside all functions, kept for the whole program). Pins are inputs by default; pinMode(pin, mode) sets INPUT, OUTPUT or INPUT_PULLUP.

int LED = 6;                 // LED on pin 6
void setup() { pinMode(LED, OUTPUT); }
void loop() {
  digitalWrite(LED, HIGH); delay(500);   // on for 500 ms
  digitalWrite(LED, LOW);  delay(500);   // off for 500 ms
}

The code checks isnan() on the readings and prints “Failed to read from DHT sensor!” if any failed, then prints humidity, temperature (C and F) and the heat index. 1State Ohm’s law and solve: a battery pushes current through 3 ohm and 12 V is applied. Then find the voltage for 2 A through 7 ohm.

V = I x R. First: I = V / R = 12 / 3 = 4 A. Second: V = 2 x 7 = 14 V.

2Three resistors 3 k, 10 k and 5 k are in series across a battery that drives 500 microamp. Find total resistance, battery voltage and each drop.

Total R = 3 + 10 + 5 = 18 kohm. Drops: 500 uA x 3 k = 1.5 V; x 10 k = 5.0 V; x 5 k = 2.5 V. Battery voltage = sum = 9 V (equally 500 uA x 18 k = 9 V, my check).

3Three resistors 10 k, 2 k and 1 k are in parallel across 9 V. Find total resistance and each branch current.

1/R = 1/10k + 1/2k + 1/1k = 0.1 + 0.5 + 1.0 = 1.6 mS, so R = 625 ohm. Currents: 9/10k = 0.9 mA, 9/2k = 4.5 mA, 9/1k = 9.0 mA; total 14.4 mA (my sum; 9 V / 625 ohm = 14.4 mA agrees).

4Series versus parallel: give one advantage and one disadvantage of each.

Series: advantage, it does not heat easily; disadvantage, one break stops everything after it. Parallel: advantage, same voltage for each branch and a fault in one branch leaves the rest working; disadvantage, it needs many wires.

5Differentiate a microprocessor from a microcontroller, and name three unique features of an embedded system.

A microprocessor is a CPU only and relies on external memory and peripherals. A microcontroller puts CPU, memory, input/output, clock and timer on one chip and runs dedicated tasks. Embedded systems are built for one task, run without human intervention, and have real-time constraints.

6A button wired with INPUT_PULLUP reads HIGH. Is it pressed? Why? And what does analogRead return for 2.5 V?

No, it is not pressed. The internal pull-up ties the pin to 5 V, so an open button reads HIGH; pressing the button connects the pin to ground and it reads LOW. analogRead gives 0 to 1023 for 0 to 5 V, so 2.5 V gives about 512 (the slide’s figure).

Part 1: connecting things to a network

A Personal Area Network (PAN) spans a few metres around a person and is common in IoT. A Local Area Network (LAN) covers a small area, is normally high speed and under one administrator. A Wide Area Network (WAN) covers a wide area and may be lower speed with parts from different Internet service providers (ISPs). An end device is the source or destination of a message; intermediary devices connect end devices and join networks; addresses identify devices; media carry the message. Ethernet rules cover communication between local devices; IP covers remote devices; routers direct packets across several networks (routing).

IoT devices are often embedded devices working in poor conditions, so they use light protocols (CoAP and MQTT, below). The slides also say IoT traffic carries traceable signatures and confidential data, so devices need a secure framework: authentication, authorization, network-enforced policy and secure analytics.

The slides explain that OSI is complex while TCP/IP is widely used, so TCP/IP is the mainstream reference model.

Part 2: wireless technologies

A wireless device sends signals as electromagnetic waves: the transmitter encodes and the receiver decodes. Modulation turns digital bits into an analog signal by changing a carrier’s frequency, amplitude or phase. There are many technologies because of trade-offs: short or long distance, high or low power, high or low speed, few or many devices, device-to-device or device-to-infrastructure, indoor or outdoor.

Part 3: the protocols on top (older deck)

Internet layer

IPv6 uses 128-bit addresses (2 to the power 128, about 3.4 x 10 to the power 38) against IPv4’s 32-bit (about 4.3 billion). They are not interoperable. IANA hands blocks to five regional registries, which give blocks to ISPs, which assign addresses to customers.

Transport layer

TCP: connection-oriented, highly reliable, time is less critical. UDP: connectionless, best effort, fast and efficient.

REST (RESTful HTTP)

The architecture style of the web: every resource has an ID (a URL, for example a sensor’s temperature), resources are linked by standard methods, communication is stateless (state is kept on the client).

CoAP

RESTful services for networked embedded devices. 4-byte header, multicast support, fits a single UDP packet or 802.15.4 frame. Methods GET, POST, PUT, DELETE like HTTP; resources by URI. Caching with the Max-Age option; proxies can cache.

Why not plain HTTP/TCP?

Constrained networks have very low power (must sleep), tiny computing resources, a need to save bandwidth, about 100 kbit/s, high loss and variable links, so TCP and HTTP are not suitable.

The next gap (slide): HTTP and CoAP struggle across firewalls (which block incoming connections and hide networks behind one address) and are not event-oriented. IoT needs one-to-many emitting, listening for events, tiny packets in huge volumes, and pushing over unreliable networks. That is the case for MQTT.

1Distinguish PAN, LAN and WAN, and say which protocols handle local and remote communication.

PAN: a few metres around a person (common in IoT). LAN: small area, high speed, one administrator. WAN: wide area, often lower speed, parts from several ISPs. Ethernet rules cover local devices; IP covers remote devices.

2Why are there so many wireless technologies? Give three trade-offs and place BLE and LoRaWAN on them.

Because of trade-offs: distance (short versus long), power (low versus high), speed (low versus high), number of devices, indoor versus outdoor. BLE: short range, low power, years of battery, up to 1 Mbps. LoRaWAN: long range, low power, low data rate of 0.3 to 50 kbps.

3Compare TCP and UDP and explain which suits IoT.

TCP is connection-oriented and highly reliable but heavy. UDP is connectionless, fast and best effort. Constrained IoT networks have low power, little computing, high loss and low bandwidth, so the slides call TCP and HTTP unsuitable and the IoT stack uses UDP.

4What is CoAP, and how does it relate to HTTP?

A RESTful protocol for constrained devices: 4-byte header, multicast support, one message fits a single UDP packet or 802.15.4 frame. It uses the same style of methods (GET, POST, PUT, DELETE) and a subset of HTTP response codes, so it translates easily to HTTP. Responses can be cached using Max-Age.

5Explain MQTT publish/subscribe, the wildcards, and the three QoS levels. What does a subscription to sensors/+/temperature/+ receive?

Publishers send messages to topics on a broker, which pushes them to subscribers; publishers do not need to know the consumers. + matches one level; # matches all remaining levels. QoS 0: at most once; QoS 1: at least once (PUBACK, possible duplicates); QoS 2: exactly once (PUBREC, PUBCOMP). The subscription receives the temperature of every device and sub-item under sensors, for example all computers and all disks.

6Give four differences between MQTT and HTTP.

MQTT is data centric, publish/subscribe, simple with a 2-byte header, has three QoS levels and supports 1-to-many. HTTP is document centric, request/response, complex and text-heavy, has one service level and is 1-to-1 only.

Part 3: security, trust and privacy

Security

Measures that protect connected devices, networks and data from unauthorised access, attacks and disruption. Challenges: many devices, secure transmission, limited processing power and memory, firmware flaws, no uniform standards.

Trust

User reliance on the security, reliability and ethical behaviour of devices and the whole ecosystem: device, data and ecosystem trust. Built by secure protocols (MQTT with TLS, CoAP with DTLS), end-to-end encryption and integrity checks (digital signatures, hash functions).

Privacy

Control over how personal data is collected, processed and shared. Key aspects: data minimisation (collect only what is needed), user consent, transparency. Concerns: profiling, weak consent, breaches, location tracking, cross-device tracking, third-party sharing, retention.
